Takeovers: how ASX developers often return capital

Many ASX developers never build their own mine. They are bought by a producer that has the balance sheet, the team and the need to replace reserves. Understanding takeover mechanics, and the signals before a bid, is a core analyst skill.

The rules (Australia)

  • 20% threshold (Corporations Act s606): you cannot acquire more than 20% of a listed company's voting shares except through permitted routes (a takeover bid, a scheme, creep, or with shareholder approval).
  • 3% creep: a holder with at least 19% can acquire up to 3% more every six months.
  • Takeover bid: an offer to all shareholders (on-market or off-market). It can be hostile. Once the bidder reaches 90%, it can compulsorily acquire the rest.
  • Scheme of arrangement: a friendly, court-approved process. It needs 75% of votes cast and 50% of holders voting (the headcount test, which courts can waive). It is all-or-nothing, the route most agreed deals take.
  • FIRB: foreign acquirers may need Foreign Investment Review Board approval, increasingly with conditions for critical minerals.
  • Deal protections: break fees (typically about 1% of deal value), no-shop and no-talk clauses, and matching rights.

Blocking stakes

▣ Case studyLiontown, Albemarle and Hancock (2023) ✔

Albemarle made several proposals for Liontown, ending at A$3.00 per share. Hancock Prospecting built a 19.9% stake, just below the 20% threshold, which was enough to block a scheme (75% of votes cast). Albemarle withdrew on 16 Oct 2023. Liontown then raised A$376m at A$1.80. A blocking holder can decide a deal without ever bidding.

Independent Expert's Reports (IER)

In schemes and many bids, an independent expert values the target and opines on whether the offer is "fair and reasonable". The IER is a free, detailed NAV, with price decks, discount rates and risk factors laid out. Read the assumptions and compare them with your own.

Premiums and arbitrage

? UnverifiedTypical premiums

Control premiums in mining deals have typically been about 25–45% to the undisturbed price unverified. NST–DEG (37.1%) sits in the middle.

∑ Key formulaMerger-arbitrage spread

Spread = (offer value − target price) ÷ target price

For scrip deals, offer value = ratio × bidder price. The spread prices time to completion, deal-break risk and bidder share-price moves.

▣ Case studyNST–DEG ✔
  • 2 Dec 2024: 0.119 NST per DEG share = A$2.08, a 37.1% premium to A$1.52.
  • DEG closed at A$1.97: a spread of (2.08 − 1.97) ÷ 1.97 ≈ 5.6%.
  • Completed 5 May 2025, about five months later, for ≈A$5bn.

Mega-deals for reference

▣ Case studyBHP–OZ Minerals ✔

BHP acquired OZ Minerals at A$28.25 per share (≈A$9.6bn), completed 2 May 2023, buying copper growth in South Australia next to Olympic Dam.

▣ Case studyNewmont–Newcrest ✔

Newmont acquired Newcrest for about US$19.1bn, completed 6 Nov 2023, creating the world's largest gold miner and removing the ASX's largest gold stock.

Thinking like an acquirer

  • Synergies: shared infrastructure, processing capacity, overheads, tax losses and funding cost. Who keeps them? Usually the target captures most through the premium.
  • Reserve replacement: producers must replace depletion. Buying is often faster than discovering.
  • Paying twice: do not add a takeover premium to a NAV that already uses the acquirer's lower cost of capital. Value optionality once.
  • Signals before a bid: a strategic stake, a standstill, a DFS just completed, a funding gap, a producer with a mill nearby.

⚑ Red flagTakeover red flags
  • Rumours used to support a placement
  • "Strategic review" announcements without a process or adviser named
  • Bids conditional on financing or many approvals
  • Targets trading above the offer with no rival bidder visible (possible over-optimism)
