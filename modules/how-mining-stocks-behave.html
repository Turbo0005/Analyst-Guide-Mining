The toy model: price × volume − cost

A mine is a factory with an odd feature: it eats itself. Every tonne sold leaves one tonne less in the ground. That makes a mining company a wasting asset. It has to keep finding, buying or extending ore just to stand still. Everything in this guide grows out of one line:

∑ Key formulaThe toy model

Margin = (Price − Unit cost) × Volume

  • Price is set by a global market you do not control (Module 13).
  • Unit cost is set by geology, metallurgy, mining method and local inflation (Modules 8–10).
  • Volume is set by the size of the orebody and the plant, and by how well the operation runs (Modules 6 and 11).

Explorers have no margin at all, only the option of a future margin. Developers have a designed margin in a study, plus a funding problem. Producers have a real margin that swings with price. That single difference explains why the same news can move three companies in three different ways.

Five kinds of mining company

Type What you own Main risks ASX examples (Oct 2026)
Explorer An option on a discovery Geology, dilution, cash runway Hundreds of sub-A$100m names
Developer A study, a permit path, a funding plan Financing, capex overruns, permitting, ramp-up Chalice (Gonneville), Hemi before the takeover
Producer Cash flow from one or more mines Commodity price, costs, grade, reserve life Northern Star, Evolution, PLS
Royalty / streamer A slice of revenue from someone else's mine Counterparty and operator risk; low cost risk Deterra Royalties; Franco-Nevada, Wheaton, Royal Gold (NYSE/TSX)
Diversified major A portfolio across commodities and countries Capital allocation, the China cycle BHP, Rio Tinto
◆ Key ideaWhere the market lists them
  • ASX has the deepest pool of junior explorers in the world, especially in gold and lithium.
  • TSX and TSX-V in Toronto are the other great junior market, governed by NI 43-101 rather than JORC (Module 6).
  • LSE/AIM hosts the majors (Rio, Anglo, Glencore) plus African and other juniors.
  • NYSE has the US majors (Newmont, Freeport) and the big streamers.

Many companies are dual-listed, so learn to read both regimes.

Operating leverage: the one formula that explains producer volatility

Why does a gold producer's share price often move about twice as much as gold? The core answer is arithmetic. If the price is P and the all-in cost is C, the margin is P − C. A price change of ΔP changes the margin by the same dollar amount, but by a larger percentage.

∑ Key formulaOperating leverage

%Δmargin = %Δprice × P / (P − C)

The multiplier P/(P − C) is large when the margin is thin. A 10% rise in price lifts margin by:

  • ~30% when the margin is a third of the price (P/(P−C) = 3).
  • ~17% when the margin is 60% of the price (P/(P−C) ≈ 1.67).
  • ~100% when the margin is a tenth of the price.
◎ Worked exampleTwo WA gold producers at A$5,750/oz

Gold is ≈US$4,050/oz, or ≈A$5,750/oz, as of 29 Oct 2026 ✔.

  • Producer A has an AISC of A$2,300/oz, so its margin is A$3,450 (60% of price). Multiplier = 5,750 / 3,450 = 1.67. A 10% gold rise (+A$575) lifts margin by 575/3,450 = +16.7%.
  • Producer B has an AISC of A$4,600/oz, so its margin is A$1,150 (20%). Multiplier = 5.0. The same 10% rise lifts margin by +50%, and a 20% fall wipes it out completely.

That is why high-cost producers rally hardest in bull markets and are the first to go to the wall in busts.

⚠ Common trapLeverage cuts both ways

The 2026 gold path is the live example. Gold set an intraday spot record near US$5,595/oz on 29 Jan 2026 ✔. By late October it was ≈US$4,050, about 28% lower as of 29 Oct 2026. A producer whose margin was 25% of a US$5,500 price had almost no margin left at the October price. Fixed costs, debt and hedges add more leverage on top, as Modules 11 and 19 show.

Why retail investors lose money in juniors

Most retail losses in junior miners do not come from a single dramatic drill miss. They come from four slow grinders:

  1. Dilution. Explorers have no revenue, so they fund every metre of drilling with new shares. Your slice of the company shrinks every year (Module 14).
  2. Delays. Timelines slip, and each year of delay costs NPV and burns cash (Modules 2 and 15).
  3. Cost blowouts. Study capex is an estimate. Real-world capex usually comes in higher (Module 15).
  4. Cycle timing. Buying near a commodity peak often wipes out good geology (Module 13).
▣ Case studyChalice Mining (ASX: CHN): a round trip
  • Chalice traded around 16–22c in early 2020.
  • On 23 Mar 2020, in the middle of the Covid crash, it reported its first Julimar hole: 19 m @ 2.59% Ni, 1.04% Cu, 8.37 g/t Pd, 1.11 g/t Pt from 48 m. The shares roughly tripled that week ✔.
  • A run of follow-up drilling took the stock to about A$4.44 later in 2020 ✔.
  • It peaked at about A$10.48 on 17 Nov 2021, after the maiden Gonneville resource ✔.
  • In Oct 2026 it trades near A$0.94, roughly 91% below the peak ✔.

The geology was real. The discovery is a large PGE-nickel-copper system. But low palladium and nickel prices, metallurgy and a long study period (the "orphan period" of Module 2) did the damage. Buyers near the peak needed the cycle and a quick path to production. They got neither.

⚑ Red flagPromoters sell the left side of the curve

Watch for releases that compare a junior to a famous discovery ("the next Hemi", "Julimar look-alike") without any comparable drilling. A comparison is not a resource.

Northern Star: the producer contrast

Northern Star Resources (ASX: NST) is a contrast to Chalice. It is a multi-mine gold producer (Kalgoorlie, Yandal, Pogo in Alaska). It is valued on cash flow and reserves, and it grows organically and by acquisition, most recently by buying De Grey and Hemi (Module 3). Its share price is mainly a function of the A$ gold price and its cost delivery, not of drill holes. When you compare an explorer's chart with a producer's chart, you are comparing an option with a cash-flow stream.
